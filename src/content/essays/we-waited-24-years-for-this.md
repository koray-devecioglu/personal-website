The alarm went off at 06:30.

Not for a flight. Not for an on-call page. For a football match.

On the morning of June 14, Türkiye kicked off against Australia at
seven o'clock Istanbul time, and across the country kettles went on
in the dark. For the first time in 24 years the national team was at
a World Cup, and if the World Cup insisted on happening at dawn, we
would be there at dawn.

I want to write down what happened next while it still stings —
partly because the internet forgets fast, mostly because the lessons
turned out to be bigger than football.

## Twenty-four years is a generation

Some context if you don't carry a Turkish passport. Türkiye has
qualified for exactly three World Cups: 1954, 2002, and now 2026. The
2002 run ended in third place and produced the fastest goal in World
Cup history — inside eleven seconds. Then: nothing. Six tournaments
came and went without us.

If you're a Turkish fan under thirty, you had never watched your
country at a World Cup. Euros, yes. Agonizing near-misses in
qualification, more than I care to count. The actual World Cup —
never.

So when the playoff final against Kosovo ended 1–0 on the last day
of March and the ticket was finally ours, the country lost its mind
a little. Deservedly. Whatever came next, we were going.

## Three mornings, briefly

**Vancouver, June 14, 07:00 our time.** Australia 2–0. A twenty-year-old
named Nestory Irankunda ran through us in the first half, a
debutant goalkeeper saved everything Arda Güler aimed at him, and a
second goal arrived from 23 meters while we were still looking for
our first shot on target that troubled anyone. We looked slow, flat,
and strangely absent — like a team that had left its legs somewhere
else.

**Santa Clara, June 20, 06:00.** Paraguay went down to ten men. We
still lost, 1–0. Two matches: zero points, zero goals, eliminated
before the group stage was even done with us.

**Then the strangest morning of the three — June 26, 05:00.**
Türkiye 3–2 against the United States. The hosts had already won the
group; we were already out; the match decided nothing. Kaan Ayhan
scored the winner with the final kick of the game, and my living room
celebrated at sunrise like it mattered.

It didn't matter. We celebrated anyway. Waking up at five for a dead
rubber and roaring at a last-second winner nobody needed — that, for
better or worse, is what being a fan is.

## It didn't start in Vancouver

The performances looked like a team problem. I don't believe they
were. I think the team was the last domino.

Consider what was arranged around them.

For months there had been questions about who fills the federation's
offices and how — whether the people running the national team's
biggest tournament in a generation were there because they were the
best available, or because they knew the right someone. The president
publicly denied stuffing the place with family and friends — the
defense included clarifying that only one nephew works at the
federation. When "only one nephew" is the reassuring version of
events, the org chart has already lost the benefit of the doubt.

And the detail that stays with me: the training camp. Reports after
the exit claimed the squad was based at a facility in Arizona that
FIFA had merely _recommended_, not mandated — and that the
federation kept it largely because changing it would cost money. Our
matches were in Vancouver and the Bay Area. Arizona in June is an
oven.

Each of these alone is survivable. Together they describe an
organization that had stopped doing its one job: putting eleven
people in a position to perform. Players don't lack energy for 180
straight minutes by accident. They walked onto the pitch at the end
of a long chain of small compromises, and every one of those
compromises was visible in the way they moved.

## What I'm taking from it (engineer brain, sorry)

I spend my working life around systems, so this is the lens I've
got.

**Performance is downstream of environment.** In software you cannot
hire your way out of a broken organization, and in football you
cannot pick your way out of one either. A squad with Champions League
starters produced zero goals in two matches not because the talent
evaporated but because talent is the _last_ multiplier in the chain,
not the first. Psychological safety isn't a soft perk — it's a
precondition of performance, in a dressing room exactly as much as
in an engineering team. Players carrying a federation's chaos on
their backs have no spare capacity left for the game.

**The happy path is not the system.** Qualification was the happy
path — one good night in March against Kosovo. The tournament was
production traffic. Software taught me that
[everything works until it doesn't](/posts/everything-works-until-it-doesnt),
and that what you learn about a system, you learn from its failure
modes. A federation, it turns out, is the same kind of system.

**Blameless doesn't mean consequence-free.** After the exit, the
loudest demand was for the coach's head. The federation refused and
talked about stability. Stability is the right instinct and the
wrong first move: you keep the coach _and_ fix the system — not keep
the coach _instead of_ fixing the system. A postmortem that ends at
"we stand by our people" with no action items isn't blameless. It's
just unfinished.

**Showing up still compounds.** Here's the part I refuse to be
cynical about. Somewhere in İzmir or Trabzon there's a ten-year-old
who got shaken awake three times in June to watch Türkiye at a World
Cup — who saw Ayhan's last kick beat the hosts at five in the
morning and doesn't yet understand what a dead rubber is. That kid
is now a fan for life. The 2002 team did that to a generation. This
team, for all its misery, just did it to another one. Absence
teaches nothing; presence — even a painful one — plants something.

## Until next time

We waited 24 years for two weeks that mostly hurt.

I'd still take it over the waiting. And when the next cycle comes
around, the fix we should be shouting for isn't a new coach or a new
scapegoat — it's a federation that treats the team the way any good
organization treats the people doing the actual work: clear the
obstacles, absorb the pressure, and get out of the way.

If the matches kick off at five in the morning again, we'll set the
alarms again. That part was never in question.
